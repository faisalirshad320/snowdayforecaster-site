Snow day glossary

The vocabulary districts, forecasters and this site use, defined once.

Plain-English definitions of the terms used across this site and in district announcements — snow day, remote day, two-hour delay, cold day, wind chill, lake-effect snow, freezing rain, sleet, ground pack, snow-water equivalent, and the scoring terms we use to report accuracy.

Snow day
A school day cancelled because of winter weather. In this site's data a snow day means the district cancelled classes outright; a remote day and a delay are counted separately.
Remote day
A day when buildings are closed but classes run online. Many districts switched to remote days after 2020 to avoid adding days to the end of the year. In the 2025-26 season we tracked, remote days were about as common as full closures.
Two-hour delay
A delayed start, usually two hours, so ploughs and salt trucks can finish and the sun can soften ice on side roads. Districts reach for a delay when the problem is timing rather than depth.
Cold day
A closure called for dangerous cold rather than snow, based on wind chill at bus-stop time. Thresholds vary widely: Chicago closed near −20°F wind chill in January 2026 while Minneapolis stayed open at −31°F.
Wind chill
How cold the air feels on exposed skin once wind is accounted for. It is the number districts use for cold closures, not the plain temperature.
Lake-effect snow
Snow formed when cold air crosses a warm lake, picks up moisture and dumps it downwind in narrow, intense bands. It can drop two feet on one town and nothing five miles away, which is why forecasts for Buffalo, Erie and Syracuse are the hardest to get right.
Freezing rain
Rain that freezes on contact with cold ground, coating roads in clear ice. A quarter inch of freezing rain closes more schools than six inches of dry snow.
Sleet
Ice pellets that bounce rather than stick. Sleet reads as almost no snow depth in weather data but makes roads just as slick, which is one reason snow-depth-only calculators miss closures.
Snow-water equivalent
The amount of water in fallen snow or ice. This site's model measures frozen precipitation in inches of water rather than inches of snow, so ice counts properly.
Ground pack
Snow and ice still lying on the ground from earlier storms. It builds with each storm and melts once highs rise above freezing. Most closures in our data happened on pack days, after the storm had already ended.
Brier score
A score for probability forecasts: the average squared gap between the forecast and what happened. Lower is better, and 0 is perfect. It rewards being well calibrated rather than confident.
Calibration
Whether forecasts mean what they say. A calibrated 70% means school actually closes about 70 times in 100 such days. We publish our calibration table rather than a single accuracy figure.
Leave-one-district-out test
Scoring each district with a model trained only on the other districts, so the result shows how the forecast performs somewhere it has never seen.
Climate tier
One of six regional groups this model uses, from the northern snow belt to the South. The tier shifts both the baseline closure rate and how strongly snow, ice and cold count.
