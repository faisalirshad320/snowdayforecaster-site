Lake-effect snow and why it breaks snow day forecasts

The one kind of storm where a ZIP-code forecast is genuinely at a disadvantage — and what to do about it.

Lake-effect snow forms when cold air crosses a warmer lake, absorbs moisture, and releases it as narrow, intense bands downwind. It can drop two feet on one town and nothing five miles away.

That narrowness is why it is the hardest closure to predict. Grid-based forecasts average the band away, so our model — and every other ZIP-code calculator — under-reads lake-effect events. We missed several Buffalo closures in back-testing for exactly this reason, and say so on the methodology page.

How a band forms

  1. Arctic air moves over a lake that is still much warmer than the air — a difference of 13°C or more between the water and the air about 1.5 km up is the usual rule.
  2. The bottom of that air mass warms and picks up moisture over a long stretch of open water, called the fetch.
  3. It rises, cools, and organises into bands aligned with the wind.
  4. The bands come ashore and unload, intensified where the land rises.

Rates of two to four inches an hour are routine inside a band. Ten feet away from its edge, nothing.

Why a grid model misses it

Forecast models resolve weather on a grid a few kilometres across. A lake-effect band can be narrower than that, and its landfall depends on wind direction to within a few degrees. The model gives the grid cell an average, which is wrong in both directions at once: too little snow for the town under the band, too much for the town beside it.

This is a real, named limit of our forecast rather than a rounding error. Buffalo is the district where our back-test performed worst, and the reason is in this paragraph.

What to do if you live in a snow belt

  • Treat the calculator's number as a floor rather than an estimate on lake-effect days.
  • Watch for a lake-effect snow warning naming your county specifically — the National Weather Service issues these at county level, which is far finer than any model grid.
  • Check wind direction. A band that sits over you for six hours is a closure; the same band twenty miles north is not.

Questions

What is lake-effect snow?

Snow that forms when cold air crosses a relatively warm lake, picks up moisture and heat, and dumps it as intense, narrow bands on the downwind shore. It needs a big temperature difference between the water and the air above it, and a long enough stretch of open water for the air to travel over.

Why is lake-effect snow so hard to forecast?

Because the bands are only a few miles wide and their position depends on wind direction to within a few degrees. A shift of ten degrees moves two feet of snow from one town to the next. Grid-based forecast models smear this out, so any calculator — including ours — under-reads it.

Which places get it worst?

The snow belts downwind of the Great Lakes: Buffalo and Watertown off Lake Erie and Lake Ontario, Erie in Pennsylvania, Cleveland's eastern suburbs, and the western side of Michigan off Lake Michigan.

Does it stop in mid-winter?

It eases once the lakes ice over, because the open water that feeds it is gone. That is why Buffalo's worst lake-effect events cluster in late autumn and early winter rather than February.
